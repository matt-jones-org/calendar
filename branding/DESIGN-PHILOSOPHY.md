# Scorecard Modern

*A design philosophy*

## The Movement

Scorecard Modern is the discipline of the pencil-kept record raised to the level of monument. It borrows its soul from the objects of a slower game: the cream of unbleached card stock, the single red seam that holds a sphere together, the green of grass mowed in patterns, the heavy condensed capitals of a stadium scoreboard. Nothing decorative survives here that does not also do work. Every mark earns its place the way a run earns its place on a scorecard — recorded once, in ink, with total conviction.

Form is circular and centered. The circle is the movement's only sacred geometry: it is the ball, the plate, the sun over a day game. Around it, space is generous and flat — wide fields of warm paper that let the central form breathe the way an outfield lets a fly ball hang. Composition is symmetric to the millimeter, because symmetry is what a fan sees looking down the foul lines from behind home plate. Balance is not approximate; it is surveyed, chalked, and measured twice, the product of painstaking attention that should be felt before it is noticed.

Color is a roster of four, no substitutions: paper cream as the field of play, card white as the object of attention, stitch red as the single line of energy, and ink near-black as the voice of record. Grass green sits on the bench, used only when the composition demands a second breath. Red is never a wash — it is always a line, a seam, a lace: the thread that literally holds the work together. This restraint must read as mastery, not economy; each hue calibrated with the care of a colorist who has mixed the same four inks for thirty years.

Rhythm comes from the stitch. The repeated tick — short, angled, evenly paced along a curve — is the movement's heartbeat, an accumulation of small identical marks that builds meaning through patient repetition, like 162 games building a season. These marks must be placed parametrically, perfectly spaced, each one perpendicular to its curve, so the eye reads craft measured in countless hours: a seam sewn by hand by someone at the absolute top of their trade.

Typography is a roar kept to one syllable. Letters are condensed, heavy, vertical capitals — scoreboard letters, jersey letters — and they appear only as monogram or label, never as sentence. Type is not set on the work; it is *of* the work, sitting inside the geometry like a number sewn on wool flannel. Whatever is written must be so short it could be stitched.

The finished object must feel like an artifact from an imaginary archive: the official mark of a club that has always existed. Flat, printable, honest at any size — legible as a distant scoreboard and rewarding under a loupe. Meticulously crafted, labored over, master-level in execution: the viewer should be certain a human hand drew every curve, and drew it a hundred times before choosing this one.
